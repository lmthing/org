# Anti-Inflammatory Diet

## Foods to Include

### Recommended

- **Fatty fish:** Salmon, sardines, mackerel, anchovies, herring, trout. The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA are metabolized into resolvins and protectins — specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) that actively resolve inflammation. Target 2-3 servings per week or 1-2 g EPA+DHA daily via supplements.
- **Colorful fruits and vegetables:** Aim for a wide spectrum daily. Key anti-inflammatory phytochemicals:
  - Anthocyanins: berries (blueberries, blackberries, cherries, strawberries), purple grapes, red cabbage
  - Carotenoids: carrots, sweet potatoes, pumpkin, tomatoes (lycopene), leafy greens (lutein, zeaxanthin)
  - Flavonoids: citrus, apples, onions, tea, cocoa
  - Sulforaphane: broccoli sprouts (highest concentration), broccoli, Brussels sprouts, kale
- **Extra virgin olive oil:** Contains oleocanthal, which has ibuprofen-like COX-inhibiting activity. Also rich in hydroxytyrosol and other polyphenols. Use as the primary cooking and dressing fat. 2-4 tablespoons per day.
- **Nuts:** Walnuts (highest in alpha-linolenic acid, an omega-3), almonds, pistachios, hazelnuts. 1-1.5 oz/day. Associated with reduced CRP and IL-6.
- **Turmeric and curcumin:** Curcumin (the active compound in turmeric) inhibits NF-kB, COX-2, and LOX pathways. Bioavailability is low without enhancement — take with black pepper (piperine increases absorption 2,000%) or in a formulated product (e.g., Meriva, BCM-95). Effective doses: 500-2,000 mg curcuminoids per day for inflammatory conditions.
- **Ginger:** Inhibits COX and LOX enzymes. 1-2 g fresh ginger or 250-500 mg ginger extract daily. Evidence strongest for osteoarthritis pain (modest reduction comparable to ibuprofen 400 mg in some trials).
- **Green tea:** Rich in epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), which inhibits NF-kB signaling. 3-5 cups per day or 300-600 mg EGCG in supplement form.
- **Whole grains:** Oats, brown rice, quinoa, barley, buckwheat. Associated with lower CRP and TNF-alpha compared to refined grains. Fiber feeds gut microbiota to produce short-chain fatty acids (especially butyrate), which strengthen the gut barrier and reduce systemic inflammation.
- **Fermented foods:** Yogurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut, miso, tempeh, kombucha. Support a diverse gut microbiome, which is increasingly linked to systemic inflammatory regulation.
- **Herbs and spices:** Rosemary, oregano, thyme, cinnamon, cloves, garlic (allium vegetables contain quercetin and allicin). Use liberally in cooking.

## Foods to Avoid or Limit

### Pro-inflammatory Foods

- **Refined carbohydrates and added sugars:** White bread, pastries, candy, soda, fruit juices, sweetened yogurt. High-glycemic foods trigger an oxidative stress response and increase inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-alpha). Limit added sugars to less than 25 g/day.
- **Red and processed meats:** Beef, pork, lamb (limit to 1-2 servings per week), bacon, sausage, hot dogs, deli meats. Heme iron and advanced glycation end products (AGEs, formed during high-heat cooking) are pro-inflammatory. Processed meats also contain nitrites and sodium.
- **Refined seed oils (in excess):** Soybean oil, corn oil, sunflower oil, safflower oil, cottonseed oil. Very high in omega-6 linoleic acid. While omega-6 is essential, the modern Western diet has an omega-6:omega-3 ratio of 15-20:1 (vs. the evolutionary ratio of 1-2:1). Replace with olive oil, avocado oil, or balanced sources.
- **Trans fats:** Partially hydrogenated oils. Directly activate inflammatory pathways. No safe level.
- **Fried foods:** French fries, fried chicken, donuts, chips. Deep frying generates AGEs and oxidized lipids.
- **Alcohol (excess):** Moderate intake (1 drink/day for women, 2 for men) may have neutral or mild anti-inflammatory effects (especially red wine). Chronic heavy alcohol use activates Kupffer cells in the liver, driving systemic inflammation and endotoxin translocation from the gut.
- **Ultra-processed foods:** Products with long ingredient lists containing additives, emulsifiers, and artificial preservatives. Emulsifiers (carboxymethylcellulose, polysorbate-80) have been shown to degrade the intestinal mucus barrier in animal studies, promoting bacterial translocation and inflammation.

## Meal Structure

- **3 meals + 1 snack per day.** Regular meals prevent blood glucose spikes that trigger oxidative stress.
- **Breakfast:** Oatmeal with berries, walnuts, and a dash of cinnamon. Or a smoothie with spinach, blueberries, ginger, and ground flaxseed. Green tea as beverage.
- **Lunch:** Large mixed greens salad with colorful vegetables, fatty fish (salmon, sardines) or legumes, olive oil and lemon dressing, quinoa. Include fermented vegetable (kimchi or sauerkraut) as a side.
- **Dinner:** Baked or grilled fish (or legume-based dish), roasted vegetables with turmeric and rosemary, whole grain (brown rice or sweet potato). Use olive oil for all cooking.
- **Snack:** Handful of mixed nuts and berries. Or apple slices with almond butter. Or a small piece of dark chocolate (>70% cocoa).

## Nutrient Targets

| Nutrient | Target | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| Omega-3 (EPA+DHA) | 1-2 g/day | From fatty fish and/or supplements. Higher end for established inflammatory conditions. |
| Fiber | 30-40 g/day | From diverse plant sources to support microbiome diversity |
| Polyphenols | Diverse, unmeasured | "Eat the rainbow" — maximize color variety from plant foods |
| Vitamin D | Maintain 30-50 ng/mL serum 25(OH)D | Vitamin D deficiency is associated with elevated inflammatory markers. Supplement 1,000-4,000 IU/day if deficient. |
| Magnesium | 310-420 mg/day | Low magnesium is associated with elevated CRP. Best sources: dark leafy greens, nuts, seeds, whole grains, dark chocolate. |
| Zinc | 8-11 mg/day | Important for immune regulation. Best sources: oysters, pumpkin seeds, legumes, nuts. |
| Curcumin | 500-2,000 mg/day (if supplementing) | Must be taken with piperine or in a bioavailable formulation |
| Saturated fat | <10% of calories | Replace with MUFA and omega-3 PUFA |

## Relationship to Conditions

- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA):** Strongest evidence for omega-3 supplementation (2-3 g/day EPA+DHA reduces tender joint count and morning stiffness by ~30% in some trials). Mediterranean-style anti-inflammatory diet is recommended by EULAR as an adjunctive approach. Combine with DMARD therapy — diet does not replace disease-modifying treatment.
- **Osteoarthritis:** Weight loss (if overweight) is the most impactful dietary intervention for knee and hip OA (each pound of weight loss reduces knee joint load by 4x). Omega-3, ginger, and curcumin provide modest pain reduction. Vitamin K adequacy (leafy greens) supports bone and cartilage health.
- **Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):** Exclusive enteral nutrition is first-line for pediatric Crohn's disease. In adults, the Mediterranean diet pattern is associated with lower disease activity. Specific anti-inflammatory nutrients: omega-3, curcumin (ulcerative colitis maintenance, 2 g/day), and prebiotic fiber (caution during active flares — may worsen symptoms).
- **Metabolic syndrome:** Chronic low-grade inflammation (elevated CRP, TNF-alpha from visceral adipose tissue) drives insulin resistance and atherosclerosis. The anti-inflammatory diet addresses both the metabolic and inflammatory components simultaneously. Weight loss of 5-10% dramatically reduces inflammatory adipokine production.
- **Cardiovascular disease:** Inflammation is the link between lipids and plaque instability. The CANTOS trial (canakinumab, an anti-IL-1beta antibody) proved that reducing inflammation without changing lipids reduces cardiovascular events. The anti-inflammatory diet aims to achieve a similar effect through dietary polyphenols, omega-3 fatty acids, and reduced processed food intake.
- **Depression and cognitive decline:** Neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized in depression and neurodegenerative diseases. The SMILES trial demonstrated that a Mediterranean-style anti-inflammatory diet significantly reduced depression scores compared to a control group. Omega-3 (especially EPA >1 g/day) has the most evidence for mood benefits.
