# Drug-Drug Interactions

## Interaction Mechanisms

### CYP450 Enzyme System

The cytochrome P450 enzyme family in the liver is responsible for metabolizing the majority of drugs. Interactions occur when one drug inhibits or induces the metabolism of another.

**Major CYP enzymes and common substrates:**

- **CYP3A4** — statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin), calcium channel blockers (amlodipine), benzodiazepines (midazolam, triazolam), macrolide antibiotics (clarithromycin), HIV protease inhibitors, some opioids (fentanyl). Accounts for roughly 50% of all drug metabolism.
- **CYP2D6** — beta-blockers (metoprolol), SSRIs (fluoxetine, paroxetine), opioids (codeine, tramadol, oxycodone), tamoxifen. Genetically polymorphic — poor vs. ultra-rapid metabolizers.
- **CYP2C9** — warfarin, phenytoin, NSAIDs (ibuprofen, celecoxib), sulfonylureas (glipizide).
- **CYP2C19** — clopidogrel, proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole), escitalopram, voriconazole. Also polymorphic.
- **CYP1A2** — theophylline, caffeine, clozapine, duloxetine. Induced by smoking and charcoal-grilled food.

**Inhibitors** slow metabolism of substrate drugs, raising their plasma levels. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors include ketoconazole, clarithromycin, ritonavir, and grapefruit juice. Even a single dose of a strong inhibitor can increase substrate exposure 5-fold or more.

**Inducers** accelerate metabolism, reducing substrate drug levels. Rifampin is the prototypical inducer (reduces substrate levels 50-90%). St. John's Wort, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and phenobarbital are also potent inducers. Induction takes days to weeks to fully manifest and persists for days after the inducer is stopped.

### QT Interval Prolongation

Many drugs prolong the QT interval by blocking the cardiac potassium channel hERG (Kv11.1). When multiple QT-prolonging drugs are combined, the risk of torsades de pointes (TdP) — a potentially fatal ventricular arrhythmia — increases multiplicatively.

**High-risk QT-prolonging drugs:**

- Antiarrhythmics: amiodarone, sotalol, dofetilide, quinidine
- Antibiotics: fluoroquinolones (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin), macrolides (azithromycin, clarithromycin)
- Antipsychotics: haloperidol, ziprasidone, quetiapine, chlorpromazine
- Antidepressants: citalopram (dose-dependent), escitalopram
- Antiemetics: ondansetron, droperidol
- Others: methadone, tacrolimus, arsenic trioxide

**Risk amplifiers:** hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia, bradycardia, female sex, congenital Long QT syndrome, heart failure, hepatic impairment reducing drug clearance. A baseline ECG and electrolyte panel should precede combining two or more QT-prolonging agents.

### Serotonin Syndrome

Excessive serotonergic activity — most often from combining multiple serotonergic drugs — causes a spectrum from mild tremor and diarrhea to life-threatening hyperthermia, rigidity, and seizures.

**Serotonergic drugs by mechanism:**

- SSRIs: fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, fluvoxamine
- SNRIs: venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine
- MAOIs: phenelzine, tranylcypromine, selegiline (including transdermal patch)
- Triptans: sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptan (mechanism debated, but labeling carries warning)
- Others: tramadol, linezolid (reversible MAOI), methylene blue, dextromethorphan, St. John's Wort, 5-HTP, tryptophan

**Key rule:** An SSRI should never be started within 14 days of stopping an MAOI. Fluoxetine (long half-life, active metabolite norfluoxetine ~7-15 days) requires a 5-week washout before MAOI initiation.

## Severity Levels

| Level | Criteria | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **Minor** | Mild, predictable effect. Patient may experience minor side effects that are self-limiting. No dose adjustment typically needed. | Document and monitor. Inform patient of possible mild effects. |
| **Moderate** | Clinically significant interaction. May require dose adjustment, additional monitoring, or a therapeutic alternative. Patient outcome could worsen if unmanaged. | Consider dose reduction of affected drug. Schedule monitoring within 1-2 weeks. Assess for alternatives. |
| **Severe** | High risk of serious harm. Hospitalization may be required. The interaction is well-documented and likely to occur. | Avoid combination if possible. If unavoidable, use lowest effective doses with close monitoring (labs, ECG, vitals). Document clinical rationale. |
| **Contraindicated** | Risk clearly outweighs any benefit. The combination has caused serious adverse outcomes including death in clinical reports or is mechanistically certain to cause harm. | Do not prescribe or dispense together. Find alternatives. If a patient is already on both, contact prescriber immediately. |

## Common High-Risk Pairs

| Drug A | Drug B | Mechanism | Severity |
|--------|--------|-----------|----------|
| Warfarin | NSAIDs (especially aspirin) | Additive bleeding via platelet inhibition + vitamin K antagonism | Severe |
| Warfarin | Fluconazole, metronidazole | CYP2C9 inhibition increases warfarin levels | Severe |
| Simvastatin | Clarithromycin, itraconazole, nefazodone | CYP3A4 inhibition causes rhabdomyolysis | Contraindicated |
| ACE inhibitors | Potassium-sparing diuretics, trimethoprim | Hyperkalemia (especially in renal impairment) | Moderate-Severe |
| Lithium | NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, thiazide diuretics | Reduced lithium clearance leads to toxicity | Severe |
| Methotrexate | Trimethoprim, sulfamethoxazole | Additive antifolate effect: pancytopenia | Severe |
| Clopidogrel | Omeprazole, esomeprazole | CYP2C19 inhibition reduces clopidogrel activation; increased cardiovascular events | Moderate-Severe |
| SSRIs | Tramadol | Serotonin syndrome risk + tramadol analgesia reduced by CYP2D6 inhibition | Moderate |
| Digoxin | Amiodarone, verapamil, clarithromycin | P-glycoprotein inhibition increases digoxin levels | Severe |
| Phenytoin | Carbamazepine | Mutual enzyme induction; both drugs subtherapeutic | Moderate |

## Monitoring Parameters

When a drug interaction cannot be avoided, establish a monitoring plan:

1. **Baseline labs** before starting the interacting combination: CBC, BMP (electrolytes, creatinine, BUN), LFTs, INR (if warfarin), drug levels (lithium, digoxin, phenytoin, theophylline), ECG (if QT risk).
2. **Timing of follow-up labs:** Most drug levels and labs should be checked 5-7 days after initiating or changing the combination, then weekly until stable, then monthly or quarterly.
3. **Patient education:** Tell the patient which symptoms to watch for specific to the interaction (e.g., muscle pain + dark urine for rhabdomyolysis, unusual bleeding for warfarin interactions, tremor/confusion for lithium toxicity).
4. **Documentation:** Record the interaction identified, the clinical rationale for proceeding, the monitoring plan, and patient counseling in the medical record.
5. **Reconciliation:** Every transition of care (admission, transfer, discharge) should trigger a full medication reconciliation focused on known interactions.
