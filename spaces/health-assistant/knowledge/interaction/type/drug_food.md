# Drug-Food Interactions

## Interaction Mechanisms

Drug-food interactions occur through several pharmacokinetic pathways:

### Absorption Interference

Food can alter drug absorption by changing gastric pH, delaying gastric emptying, binding directly to the drug, or competing for intestinal transporters. This can reduce, delay, or (less commonly) increase the amount of drug absorbed.

**Mechanisms:**
- **Chelation:** Divalent and trivalent cations (calcium, magnesium, iron, aluminum) form insoluble complexes with drugs. Tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are the classic examples — co-administration with dairy, antacids, or iron supplements can reduce absorption by 50-90%.
- **Gastric pH alteration:** Food stimulates acid production. Enteric-coated formulations may dissolve prematurely if taken with a large meal. Proton pump inhibitors should be taken 30 minutes before a meal for optimal activation of the proton pump.
- **Transporter competition:** Fruit juices (orange, apple) inhibit the intestinal OATP1A2 transporter, reducing the absorption of fexofenadine, aliskiren, and certain beta-blockers by 30-60%.
- **First-pass metabolism modulation:** High-fat meals can increase the bioavailability of lipophilic drugs (e.g., albendazole AUC increases 4.5-fold with a fatty meal) by stimulating bile flow and lymphatic transport.

### Metabolic Modulation via CYP and P-gp

Certain foods contain compounds that inhibit or induce drug-metabolizing enzymes, most notably CYP3A4 and the efflux transporter P-glycoprotein (P-gp).

## Severity Levels

| Level | Criteria | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **Minor** | Food causes a small, clinically insignificant change in drug levels (less than 20% AUC change). Patient may not notice any difference. | No special instructions needed beyond general timing guidance. |
| **Moderate** | Food alters drug levels enough to potentially reduce efficacy or increase side effects. AUC change 20-50%. Timing of administration relative to meals matters. | Counsel patient on when to take the medication relative to meals (with food, on empty stomach, or separate by 2+ hours). |
| **Severe** | A specific food can cause a major change in drug exposure (AUC change greater than 50%) or trigger a dangerous adverse effect. Well-documented clinical cases exist. | Absolute dietary restriction. Provide written patient instructions. Example: grapefruit with simvastatin. |
| **Contraindicated** | The food-drug combination has a high probability of causing serious harm and must be avoided entirely throughout the treatment course. | Document the restriction prominently. Flag in allergy/interaction records. Verify patient understanding at every visit. |

## Key Drug-Food Interactions

### Grapefruit and CYP3A4

Grapefruit contains furanocoumarins (bergamottin, 6',7'-dihydroxybergamottin) that irreversibly inhibit intestinal CYP3A4. A single glass of grapefruit juice can reduce intestinal CYP3A4 activity by 47% within hours, and the effect persists for 24-72 hours because new enzyme must be synthesized.

**Affected drugs (partial list):**

| Drug | AUC Increase | Clinical Risk |
|------|-------------|---------------|
| Simvastatin | up to 12-fold | Rhabdomyolysis |
| Lovastatin | up to 12-fold | Rhabdomyolysis |
| Atorvastatin | ~2.5-fold | Myopathy |
| Nifedipine | ~2-fold | Hypotension, tachycardia |
| Felodipine | ~2-4-fold | Hypotension, flushing |
| Tacrolimus | ~2-3-fold | Nephrotoxicity |
| Cyclosporine | ~1.5-2-fold | Nephrotoxicity |
| Buspirone | ~4-6-fold | Excess sedation |
| Carbamazepine | ~1.5-fold | Toxicity |
| Fentanyl (oral transmucosal) | ~1.5-fold | Respiratory depression |

**Important notes:** Pravastatin and rosuvastatin are not significantly affected by grapefruit (they are not CYP3A4 substrates). Seville oranges (but not sweet oranges) contain the same furanocoumarins. Pomelo and lime may have similar effects at high quantities.

### Vitamin K and Warfarin

Warfarin acts by inhibiting vitamin K epoxide reductase, depleting active vitamin K needed for clotting factor synthesis. Sudden changes in dietary vitamin K intake destabilize INR control.

**High vitamin K foods (per serving, approximate mcg):**

- Kale: 540 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Spinach: 440 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Collard greens: 530 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Swiss chard: 360 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Broccoli: 110 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Brussels sprouts: 150 mcg (half cup cooked)
- Green tea (large quantities): variable

**Counseling approach:** The key is consistency, not avoidance. Patients should maintain their usual vitamin K intake. A sudden increase (e.g., starting a "green smoothie" habit) can drop the INR and risk thrombosis. A sudden decrease (e.g., stopping all green vegetables during illness) can raise the INR and risk bleeding. Cranberry juice/cranberry products may also affect warfarin through CYP2C9 inhibition — advise moderation.

### Calcium, Iron, and Thyroid Medications

Levothyroxine and other thyroid hormone replacements have notoriously poor oral bioavailability (40-80%) that is further reduced by concurrent calcium or iron ingestion.

- **Calcium carbonate:** Reduces levothyroxine absorption by 20-30% when taken together. Separate by at least 4 hours.
- **Iron supplements (ferrous sulfate):** Reduce absorption by up to 30-40%. Separate by at least 4 hours.
- **Calcium-fortified foods** (orange juice, cereal): Same binding effect as supplements.
- **Proton pump inhibitors:** Reduce gastric acid, impairing levothyroxine dissolution. May need dose increase.

**Other relevant food-drug pairs:**

| Food/Drink | Drug | Effect | Management |
|------------|------|--------|------------|
| Dairy, calcium supplements | Tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones | Chelation reduces absorption 50-90% | Separate by 2 hours |
| High-fat meals | Efavirenz | Increased absorption may worsen CNS side effects | Take on empty stomach at bedtime |
| Tyramine-rich foods (aged cheese, cured meats, wine, soy products) | MAOIs | Hypertensive crisis (tyramine pressor effect amplified) | Strict tyramine-restricted diet |
| Green vegetables, brussels sprouts | Warfarin | Increased vitamin K reduces anticoagulant effect | Maintain consistent intake |
| Alcohol | Acetaminophen | Induces CYP2E1, increases hepatotoxic metabolite NAPQI | Limit to 2-3 drinks/day max; caution with chronic use |
| High-fiber meals | Digoxin | Reduced absorption | Take on empty stomach |
| Charcoal-grilled food | Theophylline | CYP1A2 induction, reduced levels | Consistent diet, monitor levels |
| Soy products | Thyroid medications | Interferes with absorption and may affect thyroid function | Separate by 4 hours |

## Monitoring Parameters

1. **Timing documentation:** For each food-sensitive medication, record the specific timing instructions given to the patient (e.g., "levothyroxine 30-60 minutes before breakfast, 4 hours apart from calcium").
2. **Lab monitoring:** INR (warfarin) should be checked within 1 week of any significant dietary change. Thyroid panels (TSH, free T4) should be checked 6-8 weeks after starting or stopping a major dietary calcium/iron source.
3. **Dietary history:** At each visit, ask about changes in diet, new supplements, new juice habits, and meal timing relative to medications.
4. **Patient education:** Provide a written list of foods to be consistent with or to avoid. For grapefruit restrictions, specify that the effect lasts 24-72 hours (not just at the time of dosing).
5. **Care transitions:** Medication reconciliation at admission and discharge should include food-drug interaction review, especially for warfarin, statins, and thyroid medications.
