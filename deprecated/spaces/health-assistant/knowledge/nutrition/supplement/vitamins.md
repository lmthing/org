# Vitamin Supplements

## Indications

### Vitamin D

**Primary indication:** Prevention and treatment of vitamin D deficiency (serum 25(OH)D <20 ng/mL) and insufficiency (20-29 ng/mL). Target range: 30-50 ng/mL for bone health and general health.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Osteoporosis prevention and adjunctive treatment (with calcium)
- Fall prevention in older adults (meta-analysis: 20% reduction in falls with 700-1000 IU/day)
- Possibly reduces MS risk and relapse rate (strong observational data, limited RCT confirmation)
- Adequate levels may reduce severity of respiratory infections

**Populations at risk:** Older adults, people with limited sun exposure, dark-skinned individuals (melanin reduces UVB-mediated synthesis), obese individuals (vitamin D sequestered in adipose tissue), malabsorption syndromes (celiac, Crohn's, gastric bypass), residents of northern latitudes (above 35 degrees), exclusively breastfed infants.

### Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin)

**Primary indication:** B12 deficiency, which is common and often undiagnosed, especially in older adults (10-20% over age 65).

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Treatment of megaloblastic anemia due to B12 deficiency
- Prevention and treatment of peripheral neuropathy (especially in diabetic neuropathy and B12-deficient neuropathy)
- Adjunctive treatment for elevated homocysteine (cardiovascular risk marker)
- Required supplementation with metformin (metformin reduces B12 absorption in 10-30% of patients over long-term use)
- Required for all patients on proton pump inhibitors chronically (reduced gastric acid impairs B12 release from food proteins)

**Populations at risk:** Vegans and vegetarians (B12 is only found in animal products), older adults (atrophic gastritis reduces absorption), post-gastric bypass patients (loss of intrinsic factor-producing cells), patients with Crohn's terminal ileitis, pernicious anemia (autoimmune destruction of parietal cells).

### Folate (Vitamin B9)

**Primary indication:** Prevention of neural tube defects (NTDs). All women of childbearing age should supplement 400 mcg/day.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Neural tube defect prevention: 400 mcg/day reduces NTD risk by 50-70%. Women with a prior NTD-affected pregnancy should take 4,000 mcg/day starting 1 month before conception.
- Methotrexate therapy: 1 mg/day (prescription dose) reduces methotrexate mucositis and myelosuppression without reducing anti-inflammatory efficacy.
- Hyperhomocysteinemia: 0.5-5 mg/day with B12 and B6 (mixed cardiovascular outcome data).

**Caution:** Folate supplementation can mask B12 deficiency by correcting anemia while allowing neurological damage to progress. Always check B12 before starting high-dose folate.

### Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)

**Primary indication:** Prevention and treatment of vitamin C deficiency (scurvy). Rare in developed countries but occurs in severe malnutrition, alcoholism, and restrictive diets.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- May slightly reduce duration and severity of common cold (8% in adults, 14% in children) when taken regularly — not effective as acute treatment after symptoms start.
- Enhances non-heme iron absorption when taken concurrently (e.g., iron supplement with a glass of orange juice).
- Adjunctive support for wound healing in deficient patients.

### Other Vitamins

- **Vitamin A:** Supplement only in documented deficiency or in specific populations (measles in children in developing countries reduces mortality by 50%). Avoid excess (>10,000 IU/day) — teratogenic in pregnancy, hepatotoxic in chronic excess.
- **Vitamin E:** No evidence for cardiovascular prevention (HOPE, HOPE-TO trials negative). High dose (>400 IU/day) may increase all-cause mortality and bleeding risk. Do not routinely supplement.
- **Vitamin K:** Supplement for osteoporosis (MK-7 form, 180 mcg/day) and in patients on warfarin who need stable intake — not for anticoagulant reversal (use vitamin K1/phytonadione prescription). Newborns receive vitamin K injection to prevent hemorrhagic disease.
- **B1 (Thiamine):** Supplement in alcohol use disorder (100 mg/day prevents Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome), heart failure (250 mg/day may improve LVEF), and bariatric surgery patients.
- **B6 (Pyridoxine):** Indicated for pyridoxine-dependent seizures, certain anemias, and carpal tunnel syndrome (limited evidence). Caution: doses >200 mg/day chronically cause sensory neuropathy.
- **Niacin (B3):** Prescription niacin (1-2 g/day) raises HDL and lowers triglycerides and LDL, but the HPS2-THRIVE trial showed no incremental benefit when added to statins and increased diabetes risk and serious adverse events. Largely superseded by statins and PCSK9 inhibitors.

## Dosing Ranges

| Vitamin | Deficiency Treatment | Maintenance / Prevention | Upper Limit (UL) | Toxicity Concerns |
|---------|---------------------|------------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Vitamin D3 | 50,000 IU once weekly for 8 weeks, then 800-2,000 IU/day | 800-2,000 IU/day (1,000-4,000 IU/day if obese, malabsorption, or dark skin) | 4,000 IU/day (per IOM; Endocrine Society allows up to 10,000 IU/day with monitoring) | Hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, kidney stones (at sustained doses >10,000 IU/day) |
| B12 (oral) | 1,000-2,000 mcg/day (high dose overcomes malabsorption) | 500-1,000 mcg/day (if at risk) or 6 mcg/day (RDA, adequate diet only) | No UL established (water-soluble, low toxicity) | Essentially non-toxic even at high doses |
| B12 (sublingual/intranasal) | 1,000 mcg/day sublingual; Nascobal 500 mcg intranasal weekly | 500 mcg weekly intranasal | No UL | None significant |
| Folate | 1-5 mg/day (prescription) for deficiency | 400 mcg/day (general), 1 mg/day (with methotrexate) | 1,000 mcg/day | Masks B12 deficiency; may accelerate growth of pre-existing cancers |
| Vitamin C | 500-1,000 mg/day for deficiency | 75-90 mg/day (RDA); 200-500 mg/day common supplement dose | 2,000 mg/day | GI upset, oxalate kidney stones (in susceptible individuals at >2 g/day) |
| Vitamin E | 200-400 IU/day for deficiency | 15 mg/day (RDA as alpha-tocopherol) | 1,000 IU/day (supplemental) | Increased bleeding risk, possible increased mortality at high doses |
| Vitamin A | 10,000-25,000 IU/day for deficiency (short-term) | 700-900 mcg RAE/day (RDA) | 3,000 mcg RAE/day (10,000 IU) | Teratogenic (birth defects), hepatotoxicity, pseudotumor cerebri |
| Vitamin K (MK-7) | Not for acute deficiency (use K1) | 90-120 mcg/day (RDA as K1); 180 mcg/day MK-7 for bone health | No UL for K1/MK-7 | Interacts with warfarin; otherwise very low toxicity |

## Drug Interactions

| Vitamin | Interacting Drug | Effect | Management |
|---------|-----------------|--------|------------|
| Vitamin D | Thiazide diuretics | Increased calcium reabsorption; risk of hypercalcemia in high-dose D + thiazides | Monitor serum calcium if both used |
| Vitamin D | Orlistat, bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine) | Reduced fat-soluble vitamin absorption | Take vitamin D at least 2 hours before or after orlistat |
| Vitamin D | Glucocorticoids | Increased vitamin D catabolism; accelerated bone loss | Higher maintenance dose (1,000-2,000 IU/day minimum) |
| B12 | Metformin | Reduced B12 absorption (30% of long-term users) | Check B12 annually; supplement 500-1,000 mcg/day if low |
| B12 | Proton pump inhibitors | Reduced gastric acid impairs B12 release from food | Check B12 every 2-3 years on chronic PPI; supplement if low |
| B12 | Chloramphenicol | May antagonize hematologic response to B12 | Avoid concurrent use if possible |
| Folate | Methotrexate | Folate supplementation reduces toxicity without reducing efficacy | Give 1 mg/day (prescription dose) with weekly methotrexate |
| Folate | Phenytoin, carbamazepine, primidone | Folate can reduce antiepileptic drug levels; conversely, AEDs deplete folate | Monitor drug levels; supplement 400 mcg-1 mg/day |
| Folate | Sulfasalazine | Inhibits folate absorption | Supplement 1 mg/day |
| Vitamin C | Warfarin | High-dose vitamin C may antagonize warfarin in some case reports | Avoid doses >1 g/day with warfarin |
| Vitamin C | Aluminum-containing antacids | Vitamin C increases aluminum absorption | Separate by 2-3 hours |
| Vitamin E | Warfarin, antiplatelets | Additive bleeding risk at doses >400 IU/day | Avoid high-dose vitamin E with anticoagulants |
| Vitamin K | Warfarin | Directly antagonizes anticoagulant effect | Maintain consistent dietary intake; avoid supplements unless prescribed |
| Niacin | Statins | Increased risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis | Use lowest niacin dose; monitor CK if combined |
| B6 | Levodopa (without carbidopa) | B6 enhances peripheral decarboxylation, reducing CNS levodopa delivery | Not an issue with sinemet (carbidopa/levodopa) |

## Deficiency Signs

| Vitamin | Early Deficiency Signs | Severe Deficiency Signs |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Vitamin D | Fatigue, muscle aches, bone pain, frequent infections | Osteomalacia (adults), rickets (children), fractures, severe myopathy, hypocalcemia |
| B12 | Fatigue, pallor, glossitis (smooth red tongue), constipation | Megaloblastic anemia, peripheral neuropathy (numbness/tingling in hands/feet), subacute combined degeneration of spinal cord, cognitive decline, psychiatric symptoms |
| Folate | Fatigue, pallor, glossitis, mouth ulcers | Megaloblastic anemia, elevated homocysteine, neural tube defects (in pregnancy) |
| Vitamin C | Fatigue, gingival inflammation, joint pain, poor wound healing | Scurvy: perifollicular hemorrhages, bleeding gums, loosening of teeth, ecchymoses, poor wound healing |
| Vitamin A | Night blindness, dry skin, impaired wound healing | Xerophthalmia, corneal ulcers, keratomalacia, complete blindness, immune suppression |
| Vitamin E | Mild hemolysis (in premature infants) | Peripheral neuropathy, ataxia, myopathy, hemolytic anemia (in premature infants or fat malabsorption) |
| Vitamin K | Easy bruising, prolonged PT/INR | Severe bleeding diathesis |
| B1 (Thiamine) | Fatigue, irritability, decreased appetite | Beriberi (wet: heart failure; dry: neuropathy), Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome (confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, amnesia) |
| B6 | Dermatitis, cheilosis, glossitis, depression | Peripheral neuropathy (ironically also caused by excess B6), seizures, sideroblastic anemia |

## Lab Markers

| Vitamin | Primary Lab Test | Optimal Range | Secondary Tests |
|---------|-----------------|--------------|-----------------|
| Vitamin D | Serum 25(OH)D | 30-50 ng/mL (75-125 nmol/L) | PTH (elevated in deficiency), serum calcium, phosphate |
| B12 | Serum cobalamin | >400 pg/mL (functional deficiency possible 200-400) | Methylmalonic acid (MMA) — elevated in true B12 deficiency; Homocysteine — elevated in B12 and folate deficiency; CBC (MCV >100 = macrocytosis) |
| Folate | Serum folate | >4 ng/mL (adequate); >10 ng/mL (optimal) | RBC folate (reflects long-term status better), homocysteine, CBC |
| Vitamin C | Plasma ascorbic acid | 0.4-2.0 mg/dL | Leukocyte ascorbic acid (more stable, less commonly available) |
| Vitamin A | Serum retinol | 30-80 mcg/dL | Retinol-binding protein (RBP) |
| Vitamin E | Serum alpha-tocopherol | 5-20 mcg/mL | Alpha-tocopherol:total lipid ratio (more accurate in hyperlipidemia) |
| Vitamin K | Serum PT/INR (functional) | INR <1.3 (normal coagulation) | PIVKA-II (proteins induced by vitamin K absence), serum phylloquinone |
