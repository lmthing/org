# Mineral Supplements

## Indications

### Iron

**Primary indication:** Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) and iron deficiency without anemia. The most common nutritional deficiency worldwide.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Treatment of iron deficiency anemia (microcytic, hypochromic anemia with low ferritin)
- Prevention in high-risk groups: pregnant women, menstruating women with heavy periods, blood donors, vegetarians/vegans, bariatric surgery patients, chronic kidney disease (with ESA therapy)
- Restless leg syndrome (if serum ferritin <75 ng/mL)
- Heart failure with iron deficiency (Ferric Carboxymaltose IV — FAIR-HF and CONFIRM-HF trials showed improved symptoms and quality of life regardless of anemia status)

**Populations at risk:** Menstruating women (1-3 mg iron lost per day of menstruation), pregnant women (expanded blood volume and fetal demands), vegetarians and vegans (non-heme iron has 10-15% bioavailability vs. 15-35% for heme iron), bariatric surgery patients, patients with chronic GI blood loss, CKD patients on erythropoiesis-stimulating agents.

### Calcium

**Primary indication:** Prevention and treatment of osteoporosis, always in combination with vitamin D.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Osteoporosis prevention: 1,000-1,200 mg/day (total from diet + supplement) with vitamin D reduces fracture risk by 15-20% when combined.
- Corticosteroid-induced osteoporosis: Calcium 1,500 mg/day + vitamin D 800-1,000 IU/day as baseline.
- Preeclampsia prevention: 1.5-2 g/day in women with low dietary calcium (WHO recommendation).
- Hyperphosphatemia in CKD: Calcium acetate or calcium carbonate as phosphate binders (with meals).

**Populations at risk:** Postmenopausal women, vegans, lactose-intolerant individuals avoiding dairy, patients on chronic corticosteroids, patients with malabsorption (celiac, bariatric surgery).

### Magnesium

**Primary indication:** Magnesium deficiency (commonly subclinical). Hypomagnesemia occurs in 10-20% of hospitalized patients.

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Muscle cramps (especially nocturnal leg cramps): 300-400 mg elemental magnesium at bedtime.
- Constipation (magnesium citrate, hydroxide, or oxide as osmotic laxative).
- Migraine prevention: 400-600 mg/day (Level B evidence per AAN guidelines).
- Hypomagnesemia correction: Critical for patients on diuretics, PPIs, or with alcohol use disorder.
- Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia: IV magnesium sulfate is standard of care for seizure prophylaxis.
- Cardiac arrhythmias: Magnesium supplementation (oral 300-500 mg/day) may reduce ventricular ectopy in patients with low magnesium.

**Populations at risk:** Diuretic users (loop and thiazide), PPI users (reduced absorption), alcohol use disorder, type 2 diabetes (renal magnesium wasting), older adults, Crohn's disease.

### Zinc

**Primary indication:** Zinc deficiency (rare in well-nourished populations but common in developing countries and with certain conditions).

**Evidence-backed uses:**
- Wilson's disease (zinc acetate blocks copper absorption as maintenance therapy).
- Age-related macular degeneration: AREDS2 formula includes zinc 80 mg/day (with copper 2 mg/day to prevent deficiency).
- Wilson's disease maintenance after chelation.
- Common cold: Zinc lozenges (13-23 mg elemental zinc) started within 24 hours of symptom onset may reduce duration by 1-2 days (mixed evidence).
- Wound healing in zinc-deficient patients.
- Diarrhea in children (10-20 mg/day for 10-14 days per WHO recommendation).

### Other Minerals

- **Selenium:** Indicated for selenium deficiency (rare except in TPN, severe malabsorption, or selenium-poor soil regions). 200 mcg/day is the upper range for supplementation. The SELECT trial showed that selenium (200 mcg/day) did not prevent prostate cancer and may increase diabetes risk. Do not supplement routinely.
- **Chromium:** Limited evidence for glucose control in type 2 diabetes at 200-1,000 mcg/day. Not routinely recommended.
- **Copper:** Supplementation needed with high-dose zinc (>40 mg/day for >3 months — zinc induces metallothionein in enterocytes which traps copper). Also needed in Wilson's disease zinc therapy and TPN.
- **Iodine:** Deficiency is rare in iodized salt regions. Prenatal supplementation (150 mcg/day) is recommended in areas without iodized salt or in vegan pregnancies. Excess iodine can trigger hyperthyroidism (Jod-Basedow phenomenon) or hypothyroidism (Wolff-Chaikoff effect).
- **Manganese:** Supplementation only for TPN patients and documented deficiency. Avoid excess — neurotoxicity (manganism, Parkinson-like syndrome) with overexposure.

## Dosing Ranges

| Mineral | Deficiency Treatment | Maintenance / Prevention | Upper Limit (UL) | Best Forms |
|---------|---------------------|------------------------|-------------------|------------|
| Iron (elemental) | 100-200 mg/day (325 mg ferrous sulfate = 65 mg elemental iron) | 8-18 mg/day (RDA) | 45 mg/day (supplemental) | Ferrous gluconate (12% elemental), ferrous sulfate (20%), ferrous fumarate (33%). Iron bisglycinate: better tolerated, similar efficacy. IV iron for intolerance, malabsorption, or CKD. |
| Calcium (elemental) | 1,500-2,000 mg/day (total) | 1,000-1,200 mg/day (total) | 2,500 mg/day (age 19-50), 2,000 mg/day (age >50) | Calcium citrate (absorbed with or without food), calcium carbonate (needs stomach acid — take with meals). Split doses: no more than 500-600 mg at a time for optimal absorption. |
| Magnesium (elemental) | 300-600 mg/day oral; IV for severe deficiency | 310-420 mg/day (RDA) | 350 mg/day (supplemental, not including food) | Magnesium glycinate (well-absorbed, calming), magnesium citrate (good absorption, laxative effect), magnesium oxide (poor absorption, mainly for constipation), magnesium threonate (brain penetration, limited evidence). |
| Zinc (elemental) | 25-50 mg/day for deficiency | 8-11 mg/day (RDA) | 40 mg/day | Zinc picolinate, zinc gluconate, zinc acetate (best absorbed). Take with food to reduce nausea. |
| Selenium | 100-200 mcg/day for deficiency | 55 mcg/day (RDA) | 400 mcg/day | Selenomethionine (organic form, better absorbed). Do not exceed UL long-term. |
| Copper | 1-2 mg/day (with high zinc) | 0.9 mg/day (RDA) | 10 mg/day | Copper gluconate, copper sulfate. Always supplement when taking >40 mg zinc/day. |

## Drug Interactions

| Mineral | Interacting Drug | Effect | Management |
|---------|-----------------|--------|------------|
| Iron | Levothyroxine | Reduces T4 absorption by 30-40% | Separate by at least 4 hours |
| Iron | Fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines | Chelation: reduces absorption of both by 50-90% | Separate by 2 hours |
| Iron | ACE inhibitors | Reduced ACEi absorption (case reports) | Separate by 2 hours |
| Iron | Carbidopa/levodopa | Chelation reduces levodopa absorption | Separate by 2 hours |
| Iron | Proton pump inhibitors | Reduced iron absorption (iron needs gastric acid for Fe3+ to Fe2+ reduction) | May need IV iron if oral fails with chronic PPI |
| Calcium | Levothyroxine | Reduces T4 absorption by 20-30% | Separate by at least 4 hours |
| Calcium | Fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines | Chelation reduces antibiotic absorption | Separate by 2 hours |
| Calcium | Bisphosphonates | Reduces bisphosphonate absorption (already only 1-5%) | Take bisphosphonate on empty stomach, 30 min before food or supplements |
| Calcium | Thiazide diuretics | Reduced calcium excretion; risk of hypercalcemia with high-dose supplements | Monitor serum calcium if calcium supplements >1,000 mg/day with thiazide |
| Calcium | Ceftriaxone (neonates) | Fatal precipitates in lungs/kidneys | Never co-administer IV calcium and IV ceftriaxone in neonates |
| Magnesium | Bisphosphonates | Reduces bisphosphonate absorption | Separate by 2 hours |
| Magnesium | Fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines | Chelation reduces antibiotic absorption | Separate by 2 hours |
| Magnesium | Levodopa, digoxin | Reduced absorption | Separate by 2 hours |
| Magnesium | Neuromuscular blockers | Additive neuromuscular depression | Caution in surgical patients receiving magnesium |
| Zinc | Fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines | Chelation | Separate by 2 hours |
| Zinc | Penicillamine, trientine | Zinc reduces copper absorption; these drugs also chelate copper | Therapeutic interaction in Wilson's disease; problematic if zinc taken with these drugs for other indications |
| Zinc | Iron (high dose) | Competitive absorption; high iron reduces zinc uptake | Separate by 2 hours if both needed at high doses |

## Deficiency Signs

| Mineral | Early Deficiency Signs | Severe Deficiency Signs |
|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Iron | Fatigue, pallor, decreased exercise tolerance, pica (craving ice, dirt) | Microcytic hypochromic anemia, koilonychia (spoon nails), angular cheilitis, glossitis, esophageal webs (Plummer-Vinson syndrome), restless leg syndrome, pagophagia (ice pica) |
| Calcium | Usually asymptomatic; perioral numbness, muscle cramps, tingling fingers | Tetany, seizures, cardiac arrhythmias (prolonged QT), osteoporosis, fractures, cataracts |
| Magnesium | Loss of appetite, nausea, fatigue, weakness | Muscle cramps/twitching, tremor, tetany, seizures, cardiac arrhythmias (PVCs, atrial fibrillation, torsades de pointes), hypocalcemia (refractory to calcium supplementation until magnesium corrected), hypokalemia (refractory to potassium until magnesium corrected) |
| Zinc | Impaired taste/smell, decreased appetite, impaired wound healing | Acrodermatitis enteropathica (perioral/periocular/acral dermatitis), alopecia, diarrhea, immune dysfunction, growth retardation, hypogonadism |
| Selenium | Muscle weakness, fatigue | Keshan disease (cardiomyopathy), Kashin-Beck disease (osteoarthropathy), impaired thyroid function, immune suppression |
| Copper | Neutropenia, bone abnormalities | Microcytic anemia (iron-refractory), neutropenia, bone abnormalities (osteoporosis, fractures), myelopathy (similar to B12 deficiency) |

## Lab Markers

| Mineral | Primary Lab Test | Optimal / Target Range | Secondary Tests |
|---------|-----------------|----------------------|-----------------|
| Iron | Serum ferritin (best single test for iron stores) | 30-300 ng/mL (women), 30-400 ng/mL (men). Aim for >50 in restless leg syndrome, >100 in heart failure. | CBC (MCV <80 = microcytic), serum iron, TIBC, transferrin saturation (TSAT <20% suggests deficiency). For CKD: TSAT >20% and ferritin >100 (or >200 per KDIGO). Reticulocyte count (response to therapy: should increase in 48-72 hours). |
| Calcium | Serum corrected calcium (adjusted for albumin) | 8.5-10.5 mg/dL (total); 4.5-5.1 mg/dL (ionized) | Ionized calcium (more accurate in acid-base disorders, hypoalbuminemia), PTH, 25(OH)D, 24-hour urine calcium (to assess adequacy in osteoporosis), phosphate. |
| Magnesium | Serum magnesium | 1.7-2.2 mg/dL | Ionized magnesium (more accurate, less commonly available), 24-hour urine magnesium (fractional excretion >4% in deficiency suggests renal wasting; <2% suggests GI losses). Note: serum magnesium may be normal while intracellular magnesium is depleted. |
| Zinc | Serum zinc | 70-120 mcg/dL | Alkaline phosphatase (zinc-dependent enzyme — decreases in deficiency), albumin (zinc is albumin-bound — low albumin lowers serum zinc without true deficiency). |
| Selenium | Serum or whole blood selenium | 70-150 ng/mL (serum) | Glutathione peroxidase activity (functional selenium marker), selenoprotein P. |
| Copper | Serum copper | 70-140 mcg/dL | Ceruloplasmin (copper is bound to ceruloplasmin — low ceruloplasmin suggests Wilson's disease or deficiency), 24-hour urine copper (elevated in Wilson's disease). |
